What’s cooking at CNI?

Prof. Satish Mulleti’s Visit and Talk on Next-Gen ADC Architectures

In July 2025, the Center for Networked Intelligence (CNI) hosted Prof. Satish Mulleti from IIT Bombay, who visited from May 10th to 27th. During his stay, he engaged in insightful one-on-one discussions with IISc faculty members and delivered a talk on May 13th titled "New Trends in High-Dynamic Range Analog-to-Digital Converters."


The seminar provided a tutorial-style overview of emerging modulo-folding-based ADC architectures that enable efficient digitization of high-dynamic range (HDR) signals using lower-power, low-dynamic range ADCs. Prof. Mulleti explained the mathematical underpinnings of the folding and unfolding processes, discussed hardware trade-offs, and drew connections with classical sampling methods like delta and sigma-delta modulation. The session concluded with a case study highlighting the method's practical advantages and limitations in communication systems.

 

Summer School on Rumours, Consensus, and Epidemics on Networks

We were fortunate to organize a five-day summer school titled "Rumours, Consensus, and Epidemics on Networks" from July 14–18, 2025, led by Prof. Ayalvadi Ganesh from the University of Bristol. The workshop attracted enthusiastic participation from students and researchers across India, who gathered at IISc to explore the dynamics of random processes on networks.


Prof. Ganesh covered foundational techniques used to analyze decentralized algorithms for large-scale systems such as the Internet, peer-to-peer networks, and blockchains. With applications spanning computer science, social dynamics, economics, and infectious disease modeling, the sessions offered both theoretical depth and practical insights into how randomness can enable robust, scalable mechanisms for consensus and information dissemination.

 

CNI Scholar Srinivas Nomula, presents at INFOCOM 2025

CNI scholar Srinivas Nomula, recently presented his research at IEEE INFOCOM 2025 in London, showcasing his work titled "The Power of Two in Large Service Marketplaces." This work explores the dynamics of a distributed service marketplace where servers independently set prices and jobs arrive with private valuations. While traditional systems rely on centralized pricing and matching, the work examined whether similar efficiency and scalability could be achieved through decentralized mechanisms.


The key finding reveals that allowing jobs to choose between two randomly selected servers—rather than one—significantly improves system performance. This "power of two" approach reduces blocking probability and stabilizes pricing even under high job arrival rates. The work demonstrates the existence and convergence to a mean field equilibrium, highlighting the strategic interaction among servers and the benefits of limited competition in large-scale service platforms. The results were supported by simulations across diverse operating scenarios, drawing strong interest at the conference.
