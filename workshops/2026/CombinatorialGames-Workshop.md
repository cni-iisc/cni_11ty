       data-abstract="Chess, Go, and Tic-Tac-Toe are examples of recreational combinatorial games. We develop axioms that allow us to study their underlying mathematics. This 2 + 2 hour session combines theory, play, and hands-on analysis. We introduce fundamental ideas from Combinatorial Game Theory (CGT) through playing a galimaufry of combinatorial games, including Nim, Hackenbush, Clobber, Toppling Dominoes, Domineering, Col, Snort, and more, moving back and forth between play, analyzing positions, and developing the underlying theory.The session begins with a glimpse of the remarkable depth of the subject. Through a generalization of Dedekind sections to games, Conway's classical book On Numbers and Games (1976) revealed a landscape of games, some of which are numbers, later popularized by Knuth's book Surreal Numbers. The real number line is merely a microscopic fragment of all these games; in Cantor's ordinal arithmetic, 1 + ω = ω, whereas in Conway's game landscape, 1 + ω > ω, although in both cases ω is greater than every real number. We then turn to ideas that form the foundation of the session, including perfect-play outcome classes, the hierarchy of game values, comparison of games, and the disjunctive sum. Top-down induction provides a central tool for analysing games and proving theorems. We also encounter the CGT-temperature scale and see how it captures the strategic importance of moving first in a particular component.
       The emphasis will be on the interaction between theory and practice. Participants play games, analyze positions, and use the theory to explain what they observe, and, if we are lucky, we formulate some conjectures. No previous knowledge of Combinatorial Game Theory is assumed. This is a compressed version of a course I have taught every year since I started at IIT Bombay in 2022. In that course, students develop their own rulesets and then study the mathematics arising from their games. If time permits, we look at some of these student-created games, and we also encourage participants to invent and explore a ruleset of their own."

       data-talktitle="Combinatorial Games in Probability and Mathematical Logic"
       data-abstract="There are two distinct categories into which my lectures on this topic can be divided:
       i) Two-player combinatorial games played on random premises: These include, on one hand, the normal, misère and escape games played on rooted trees, including rooted Galton-Watson trees (which are an excellent example of rooted random trees), and on the other, percolation games played on infinite lattice graphs. The primary objective here is to understand / analyse the probabilities of the various outcomes possible (win for the first player, loss for the first player, and, where feasible, draw for both of the players), and examine any phase transition phenomena that may arise (such as when the probability of draw goes from being equal to 0 for a certain regime of values of the parameter(s) involved in defining the game or the premise on which the game is being played, to being strictly positive in a different regime).
       ii) The Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games: These serve as a bridge between mathematical logic and combinatorial games -- in particular, when we focus on first order logic, these games help us establish, rigorously, whether two given graphs are elementarily equivalent or not. For the sake of these lectures, I shall keep my focus on first order logic alone. For a given positive integer k and given graphs G and H, if Duplicator wins the k-round Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game, played on G and H, against Spoiler, then this goes to show that for every first order sentence A of quantifier depth at most k, either A is true for both G and H, or it is false for both G and H. In my lectures, I shall try to describe some strategies that can be employed by Duplicator to ensure her victory."

       data-talktitle="Game Theory through Discrete Mathematics: Voting, Matching, and Strategic Choice"
       data-abstract=" These lectures provide an introduction to selected topics in game theory in which discrete mathematics and combinatorial ideas play a central role. The lectures are intended for Master's students with no previous background in game theory. The basic concepts will therefore be introduced from the beginning, mainly through simple examples, before moving to some fundamental results and applications.
       The first part will introduce strategic interaction and preference aggregation, with particular attention to voting. We will discuss how individual rankings can be combined into a collective decision, why apparently natural voting rules may lead to difficulties, and how strategic considerations arise. Topics may include majority voting, the Condorcet paradox, the idea behind Arrow's impossibility theorem, and strategic voting, with a brief discussion of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem.
       The second part will focus on matching problems, where agents on different sides of a market have preferences over one another. Examples include matching students to colleges and workers to firms. We will introduce the notion of stability, blocking pairs, and the deferred acceptance algorithm. The Gale–Shapley existence theorem for stable matchings will be presented, together with the main intuition behind the algorithm and some of its basic properties. If time permits, we will also discuss extensions such as many-to-one matching and matching with couples.
       Throughout the lectures, the emphasis will be on the discrete structure underlying these problems: rankings, permutations, graphs, assignments, and finite algorithms. The aim is not to provide a comprehensive treatment of game theory, but to show how relatively simple combinatorial models can be used to study important questions about collective decisions, incentives, and allocation."
